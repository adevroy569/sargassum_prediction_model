What Sargassum is, and why it matters

Sargassum is a brown seaweed that never attaches to the seabed. It drifts its whole life and grows as it travels. Since 2011 the Great Atlantic Sargassum Belt has bloomed every year from West Africa to the Caribbean, and the trade winds push part of it onto Puerto Rico.

Thick mats smother seagrass and reef, trap turtle hatchlings and strip oxygen from nearshore water. On the beach the pile rots and releases hydrogen sulfide and ammonia, enough to cause headaches, nausea and breathing trouble, and to close beaches and schools for weeks.

Knowing where it will be in five days, how much lands on each stretch of coast and how much gas it gives off lets towns stage equipment and clear beaches before the smell starts. That is what this forecast is for.

How the forecast is made

Satellite images (the USF/NOAA Floating Algae Index) are converted to wet biomass, which is carried forward on CariCOOS HF-radar currents and WRF wind forecasts. Rafts that reach the coast strand onto one of the shoreline segments of about 5 km. Fourteen Sargassum traps at La Parguera, measured weekly since 2020, are what the model is trained and checked against.

Which coasts get hit, and when, is the reliable part. Tonnages carry wide uncertainty and the gas figures are order-of-magnitude, so trust the ranking of beaches more than the numbers. Currents after day three are held at their recent average, so the last two days are indicative only.
