What Sargassum is, and why it is worth forecasting

Sargassum is a brown seaweed that floats in mats on the open ocean. Unlike almost every other seaweed it never attaches to the seabed. It spends its entire life drifting, and it reproduces by breaking apart, so a mat keeps growing as it travels. Out at sea this is a good thing. Those mats shelter fish, crabs and juvenile sea turtles, and they are the floating nursery that gives the Sargasso Sea its name.

Since 2011 a second population has appeared. The Great Atlantic Sargassum Belt now blooms every year in a band stretching from West Africa to the Caribbean, and it is the largest macroalgae bloom on Earth. The trade winds and the North Equatorial Current push part of it westward, and a share of that arrives on Caribbean shorelines including Puerto Rico.

What lands is more than an eyesore. Thick mats smother seagrass beds and shallow reef, trap turtle hatchlings, and pull the oxygen out of nearshore water. Once the pile is on the beach, bacteria break it down without oxygen and release hydrogen sulfide, the rotten egg gas, along with ammonia. On badly affected beaches this is enough to cause headaches, nausea and breathing trouble, and it closes beaches, hotels and schools for weeks at a time.

Knowing where the seaweed is today is useful. Knowing where it will be in five days, roughly how much of it will come ashore on a given stretch of coast, and how much gas that mass will give off as it decomposes is what lets a town stage equipment, warn residents and clear a beach before the pile starts to smell. That is what this site is for.

How the forecast is made

Satellite reflectance from the USF and NOAA Alternative Floating Algae Index is converted to wet biomass, then carried forward with observed surface currents from the CariCOOS high frequency radar network and forecast winds from the CariCOOS WRF model. Rafts that reach the nearshore zone strand onto one of the coastal segments of about 5 km each. Gas release is calculated from the stranded mass on a conserved yield basis, so the total released always matches the mass that landed, and the resulting areal flux is checked against published field measurements.

Fourteen physical Sargassum traps at La Parguera have measured actual shoreline influx every week since 2020. Those measurements train the model and correct its bias. Segments that host a trap are served by the trained model and are marked as such. Everywhere else is physics plus a single global correction factor.

Spatial pattern, meaning which coasts get hit and when, is the reliable part. Absolute tonnage carries wide uncertainty, and the gas figures are order of magnitude estimates. Treat the ranking of beaches as far more dependable than the absolute numbers. Currents past day three are a persistence forecast, so the last two days of the window are indicative only.
