What Sargassum is, and why it is worth forecasting

Sargassum is a brown seaweed that never attaches to the seabed — it spends its whole life drifting, reproducing by breaking apart, so a mat keeps growing as it travels. Since 2011 the Great Atlantic Sargassum Belt has bloomed every year from West Africa to the Caribbean, the largest macroalgae bloom on Earth, and the trade winds push a share of it onto Puerto Rico.

What lands is more than an eyesore. Thick mats smother seagrass and reef, trap turtle hatchlings, and strip oxygen from nearshore water. On the beach, bacteria break the pile down without oxygen and release hydrogen sulfide — the rotten egg gas — along with ammonia, enough to cause headaches, nausea and breathing trouble, and to close beaches and schools for weeks.

Knowing where it will be in five days, how much lands on a given stretch, and how much gas that mass gives off is what lets a town stage equipment and clear a beach before the pile starts to smell. That is what this site is for.

How the forecast is made

Satellite reflectance (USF/NOAA Alternative Floating Algae Index) becomes wet biomass, which is carried forward on observed CariCOOS HF-radar currents and forecast CariCOOS WRF winds. Rafts reaching the nearshore strand onto one of the coastal segments of about 5 km. Fourteen Sargassum traps at La Parguera have measured actual influx weekly since 2020, and are what the model is trained and bias-corrected against.

The equations

Spatial pattern — which coasts get hit and when — is the reliable part. Absolute tonnage carries wide uncertainty and the gas figures are order-of-magnitude. Treat the ranking of beaches as far more dependable than the numbers. Currents past day three are persistence, so the last two days are indicative only.
